Atlas of the New World

10 Mar – 30 Jun 2026

When Europeans arrived in America, they had to invent a new way of representing the world. This exhibition brings together the first maps of the American continent: from the initial sketches full of uncertainty to the detailed atlases of the 17th century.

1492

Columbus's Error

Columbus reaches Guanahani believing he has arrived in the 'Indies'. In his mind and sketches, America does not exist yet; it is only the eastern edge of Asia.

1500

Juan de la Cosa Map

The first map to show American lands. Painted on parchment, it depicts the Caribbean and a continuous coastline that challenged the logic of Asian routes.

1502

Cantino Planisphere

A 'pirated' Portuguese map that shows for the first time the coast of Brazil and the line of the Treaty of Tordesillas, dividing the world between two powers.

1507

Universalis Cosmographia

Martin Waldseemuller names the continent 'America' in honor of Amerigo Vespucci. It is the first time it is represented as a landmass separate from Asia by an ocean.

1513

Piri Reis Map

The Ottoman admiral creates an astonishingly accurate map of the South American coasts, based, according to him, on lost charts by Christopher Columbus.

1519

Magellan and the Passage

Cartography breaks open to the south. The Strait of Magellan is discovered, confirming that America has an end and that a vast ocean (the Pacific) lies beyond.

1524

Map of Tenochtitlan

Published with Hernan Cortes's letters, it shows for the first time an organized American metropolis, astonishing Europe with Aztec sophistication.

1529

Padron Real (Diego Ribero)

Considered the first scientific map. Ribero outlines the coasts of Central and South America with near-modern precision based on Casa de Contratacion navigation logs.

1562

Diego Gutierrez Map

The largest and most detailed map of its time. Filled with illustrations of cannibals, Patagonian giants and mermaids, it blends science with mythology.

1570

Theatrum Orbis Terrarum

Abraham Ortelius publishes the first modern atlas. America already appears with its iconic shape, though its northwest (Alaska/Canada) remains imaginary.

1606

Mercator-Hondius Map

Jodocus Hondius refines Mercator's projection. North America begins to gain detail thanks to French and English explorations.

1700

California as an Island

A milestone of 'retro-cartography': for decades, maps wrongly represented California as an island, a myth that persisted well into the 18th century.
